## The big correction (I had this wrong before)
Contextual interference ("random practice beats blocked for retention") is **NOT** established in
applied sport settings. Four independent 2023-2024 meta-analyses:
- Czyż et al. 2024, Sci Reports (54 studies, n=2068): retention SMD 0.63 overall BUT
  **lab SMD 0.92 vs applied SMD 0.23, p=0.24 (non-significant)**. Only 3 of 54 studies were
  moderate/high quality. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11237090/
- Czyż, Wójcik, Solarská 2024, Front Psychol (transfer): overall SMD 0.55; **lab 0.75 vs applied
  0.37 (p=0.06, ns)**; young participants 0.12 (ns). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11349744/
- Ammar et al. 2023, Educational Research Review ("the myth of contextual interference"):
  36 studies, **only 37/183 pooled outcomes (20%) matched the paradoxical CI pattern**.
- Ammar et al. 2024, Educ Psych Review — critical review, same conclusion. Active dispute with
  commentaries + SportRxiv rebuttals. **Genuinely contested; do not cite CI as settled.**
- Alpine ski racers (Front Bioeng 2022): no CI effect in a real complex sport task.
- **There is NO climbing-specific contextual interference study.** Verified absent.

=> So "rotate the drill every session because random practice retains better" is NOT supported.
   Do not make that argument.

## What IS well supported (bigger effects than CI)
- **Distributed > massed practice: d ≈ 0.46-0.54.** Donovan & Radosevich meta. This is the most
  robust scheduling effect found. Short blocks across many sessions >> one long block.
- **Fatigue impairs skill LEARNING, not just execution.** Branscheidt/Celnik, eLife 2019, n=121:
  fatigued Day-1 group reached only 68% of non-fatigued performance and the deficit persisted
  ~2 further sessions *while unfatigued*. Also appeared in the non-fatigued hand (central
  mechanism). => Skill work goes FIRST, always. https://elifesciences.org/articles/40578
- **Motor imagery / visualization: g ≈ 0.48, and 0.57 combined with physical practice.** Larger
  and better evidenced than any practice-schedule trick.
- **Observing a real model is the single biggest lever on flash success** (see below).

## Weak / small effects — don't build a program around these
- External focus of attention: SMD only 0.24-0.31; distal external 0.59 but **unclear in novices**
  (Zang et al. 2025, 20 RCTs, n=497, I²=59-80%). Cue wording is a nudge, not a lever.
- Differential learning: d = 0.34, high bias risk. Notably, **structured variants + correction beat
  pure DL** — supports coached drills over random noise. (Tassignon et al. 2021, Front Psychol)
- Self-controlled practice: after bias correction **not distinguishable from zero** (McKay et al.).
- **Guidance hypothesis (less feedback = better retention) is NOT supported** by the 2022
  meta-analysis — no acquisition→retention reversal. Don't repeat that claim.
- CLA/nonlinear pedagogy: 9 studies; **0 outcomes favored linear** for technique, 34% favored
  nonlinear, but clear advantage only for TACTICAL outcomes (62.5-66%). Interventions averaged
  15.8 sessions / 7 weeks. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12321719/

## Climbing-specific (the real literature — Seifert / Orth / Hacques / Davids)
- **Hacques et al. 2022, PLOS One** (n=21 novices, 10 sessions/5wk, constant vs imposed-variable vs
  self-controlled): constant practice was BETTER on the trained route; **transfer fluency did not
  differ**; but variable practice produced **proactive gaze that transferred**, while constant
  practice specialized gaze and reverted on a new route. => Variability buys route-READING, not
  smoothness. https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0269794
- **Seifert et al. 2018** (n=8 intermediate 5c, 4 sessions/2wk, hold-orientation manipulation):
  改善 emerged by session 3-4; **4 sessions insufficient for more complex action modes**.
- **Orth et al. 2018** (n=7 beginners, 14 sessions/7wk, 42 trials): three learning trajectories
  (continuous / sudden / none). **Prior movement repertoire determined which** — individual
  differences dominate. Narrow initial repertoire → sudden jumps.
- **Morenas et al. 2021** (n=21 advanced 7b-7c+, 18 boulders at **6c**): top rate
  **no-preview 18.25% → video 42.86% → watching a real climber 61.11%** (OR 7.04 real vs none).
  => The biggest single lever on flashing 6c is watching someone climb it. Not a drill.
- Flash/onsight determinants: route interpretation + movement-sequence recall; "route preview
  errors are one of the major reasons for falling."
- **Blagus et al. 2024, RCT** (n=26 novices, 3 coached sessions): video analysis + expert modelling
  added to verbal feedback helped **high step only** (0.38, CI 0.06-0.68); drop knee ns; heel hook
  ns. => Video helps some skills, not all. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/17479541231152548
- **Langer, Andersen & Stien 2024, PLOS One RCT** (n=31 female climbers, 5wk): on-wall progressive
  bouldering on a **Kilter 15°** — 4 problems of rising difficulty, ≤4 attempts / 5 min, 2x/wk,
  ~30 min — improved *rated technique* (movement initiation, accuracy) vs control. **No drills.**
  Underpowered (27 finishers vs 52 needed).
- Motor skills retain well over long layoffs (implicit ones especially) => **a deload week will not
  cost you a drill.**

## Cognitive load / stacked constraints
- Buszard et al. 2017 (n=90 children, 5 simultaneous instructions): lower-WM group **got WORSE**
  (-5.5) while higher-WM improved (+5.6). Children, so indirect for adults — grade as moderate.
- CLA literature itself: constraints interact non-linearly; manipulating one has unpredictable
  knock-on effects. => Stacking 3 constraints makes the effect unattributable and uncontrollable.
- Whole vs part practice (Fontana et al. 2009 meta): part practice suits **high complexity + LOW
  organization**. Climbing movement is HIGH organization (components temporally coupled)
  => favors whole-task practice with a constraint, NOT isolated part drills.

## Coach positions (verified first-hand)
- **Bechtel / Climb Strong — the money quote, and he's the owner's chosen system:**
  "isolate one skill, do it for ten minutes per training session, and repeat that practice for
  **ten sessions** before moving on." https://www.climbstrong.com/resource-posts/boulder-strength-skill-sessions
  Matches his adaptation-in-8-10-sessions rule. Also: skill work early, after warmup; three
  session formats; Skill-Strategy-Strength format = ~20 min, two skill exercises (one on-wall,
  one off), then harder problems where the skill applies.
- Climb Strong fundamental skills ladder: **Balance → Precision → Execution**, with drills mapped
  (Heavy Feet, Hip Shapes / One Touch, Hover, Dead Stops / Perfect Repeats, 3 Strikes, Limit
  Bouldering). https://www.climbstrong.com/resource-posts/fundamental-skills-precision-execution-and-balance
  NOTE: catalog attributes these to Power Company; Climb Strong publishes them too. Shared vocabulary.
- Bechtel via Coyle: "SAP" = smallest achievable perfection — break it down, **work on one thing
  only** until noticeable improvement; slow movement to avoid ingraining errors; practice mindset
  is calm/absorbing, opposite of training. Bechtel & Bridgewater also wrote *The Drill Manual*
  (~30 drills, with regressions/progressions) — content not online.
- **Kris Hampton / Power Company — Atomic Elements.** Correct structure is **3+2, not a flat five**:
  "the three Inherent Elements are: Tension, Position, and Rhythm. The two Regulatory Elements are:
  Commitment and Effort." (Precision was cut — it's a *product* of Position+Tension.)
  https://www.powercompanyclimbing.com/blog/better-framework-for-movement
  Key definitional split: "Technique is the move… Skill is the ability to adapt those techniques to
  a variety of situations." Maps to Katiyo's RIC: Risk→Commitment, Intensity→Effort,
  Complexity→Tension+Position+Rhythm.
- **Hampton is NOT anti-drill** (I had this wrong). He is anti-*arbitrary constraint* and
  anti-*difficulty stacking*: "there are just too many coaches who assume that harder equals better.
  They just keep stacking difficulties onto their drills… climbers bouncing one-legged on a Bosu ball
  balanced on a slackline while blindfolded… WHY?" The filter is Bjork's **desirable difficulties**
  + **perception-action coupling**: "We can find creative ways to increase the tension required…
  without uncoupling a climbing movement from having an actual climbing component."
  https://www.powercompanyclimbing.com/blog/tt-difficulties-should-be-desirable
- His drill filter: "I've always been skeptical of the drills I see that seem to add in random
  constraints. There's a case to be made for **Differential Learning, but that still seems like too
  much of a crapshoot to apply broadly**… every movement drill should directly challenge one (or
  more) of these Elements." https://www.powercompanyclimbing.com/blog/movement-practice-vs-performance
- The "bag of tricks" critique of drill-collecting: "It's all disjointed. Like we're stuffing things
  into a bag of tricks instead of working towards a unified, sensible understanding." And:
  "a thematic framework… will be far better than the one-drill-or-one-technique-at-a-time approach."
  => DIAGNOSTIC-first: assess which Element limits YOU, then pick drills. Individual techniques are
  "not the starting point. Not even close."
- **Hampton's dosing (verified, TBP 137)**: drills go in the **warm-up or top of the warm-up zone**,
  **every single session**; "No" hard rule of days/weeks. Progression = new skill on easiest terrain
  → keep it "slightly uncomfortable" → harder terrain → performance. **Exit criterion is automaticity
  ("recognizing them in the wild"), not a session count.** Explicit hierarchy with automaticity
  gates: Rooting legs → Rooting arms → Dead Stops (Hovers lower in hierarchy). His own examples use
  **2-3 drills at a time**, one attentional cue each. Perfect Repeat: "It's ok to do the same
  Perfect Repeat problems from session to session until you're certain it can't get better."
  Difficulty anchor for most drills: **just below flash level**.
- Their session template is 3 rotating types: **Go Hard / Do More / Explore**. Drills live in Go
  Hard's warm-up; movement *exploration* gets its own session type. NOTE: "Movement Exploration" is
  a **session type, not a drill** — the catalog models it as a drill.
- "Beta > Macrobeta > Microbeta": macrobeta IS the five Elements; don't chase microbeta except on
  your hardest projects.
- They cite CLA, **Rob Gray**, Bernstein ("repetition without repetition"), Bjork, Udo Neumann.
  They **reject** differential learning as a broad prescription. They **never cite contextual
  interference**, and Hampton's cueing is largely **internal/body-focused** — i.e. contrary to the
  external-focus literature. Don't attribute either to them.
- **Kettle** stacks constraints deliberately (Big Three = Hip and Hand + Two Feet On + Straight
  Arm; Final Exam I adds Can-Can; Final Exam II uses Inside-Outside Can-Can). His stated frame is
  "if you can hold all three, the pattern is yours" — a **TEST**, not a teaching tool. Could not
  verify his published rationale beyond the book; book content not online.
